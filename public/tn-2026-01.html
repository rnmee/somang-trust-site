Why We Didn’t Build Another Brick in the Wall

A measurement-first protocol for air quality management in a small on-premise AI server room

Author Mee Chung Kim, RN — Founder & CEO Location Columbia Falls, Montana Date August 2026 Status Protocol · data collection in progress
Abstract

Standard guidance for converting a small outbuilding room into an AI server room prescribes construction first: partition walls, industrial floor coatings, premium wall systems. We evaluated that prescription against the measured characteristics of our actual facility — a 1996 site-built structure on a poured-concrete foundation — and declined it. This note documents the decision rationale, the low-cost sealed-envelope interventions adopted instead, and the continuous monitoring protocol that will verify the decision with data rather than assumptions. Construction remains available as an escalation step; it is no longer the default. Estimated capital preserved: several thousand dollars, redirected to compute.

Field measurements are appended below as milestones are reached (Appendix A).

01The facility, as it actually is

Generic server-room guidance assumes a generic room. Ours is specific. The host structure was built in 1996 on a poured-concrete foundation — a site-built outbuilding, not a mobile unit. We are its third owners. The candidate server room is an enclosed 8 × 8 ft interior room with finished walls and its own doorway; for most of our tenure it served as ski storage and remained, by long observation, essentially dust-free. That history describes the enclosed room’s past, not its present floor: with the carpet now removed, the exposed concrete sheds fines and will continue to until it is smoothed, sealed, and deep-cleaned — which is why those steps precede any hardware. The room already contains an interior electrical subpanel and a recently installed cold-climate ductless heat pump (12,000 BTU/h inverter unit, rated to −22 °F), deliberately upsized from the installer’s 9,000 BTU/h recommendation to create thermal headroom. A humidistat installed by a previous owner attests to a history of humidity awareness in the building. The adjacent former hot-tub area was decommissioned and was never plumbed to a sewage connection, eliminating a common legacy moisture pathway.

The projected heat load is a single workstation-class AI compute node of roughly 1.6 kW at the wall — about 5,500–5,800 BTU/h — plus minor room gains. Against 12,000 BTU/h of installed cooling, the room carries more than 30% capacity margin at full load, with dehumidification as a side effect of cooling-mode operation. That benefit is seasonal: Montana winters run the unit in heating mode, where the risk inverts to over-dry air and elevated electrostatic potential, so the monitoring protocol enforces a humidity floor as well as a ceiling.

02The default prescription, and what it ignores

Early advisory passes — including our own first-round specification — converged on a familiar bill of work: build an interior partition wall to isolate the room from the surrounding storage bay, coat the floor in industrial epoxy, and finish the walls in premium panel systems. The prescription is not wrong in general. It is wrong here, because it prices in assumptions our facility does not exhibit: an unenclosed space (ours is enclosed), a dust-generating environment (two decades of clean storage history), no climate control (a dedicated heat pump is on the wall), and no static mitigation plan (one exists, below).

Defaults are how small operators overspend. The instinct to add “another brick in the wall” is easy to sell precisely because it requires no measurement. We elected to measure.

03The measurement-first alternative

In place of construction, we adopted a sealed-envelope-plus-monitoring protocol in four layers:

  • Seal the existing envelope. A gasketed, swept solid-core door — replacing the previously removed leaf — restores the room’s one missing barrier; weatherstripping and acoustic-grade caulk close remaining seams and penetrations. Total materials cost is comparable to a tank of gas.
  • Prepare the surfaces. Carpet has been removed. The room will be thoroughly de-dusted, deep-cleaned, and painted in a premium low-VOC eggshell finish (Sherwin-Williams SW 7065 Argos or SW 7015 Repose Gray), Electrostatic risk is addressed at the point of contact and in the air — grounded ESD work mats and wrist straps at the service position, plus the wintertime humidity floor noted above — rather than by coating architectural surfaces with products not rated for them. The smoothed slab receives a low-VOC concrete sealer so exposed concrete stops shedding fines; industrial ESD epoxy remains unnecessary for this duty cycle.
  • Monitor continuously, locally. Following our internal case-study architecture for AI-driven on-premise air quality management, the room will carry continuous sensing of particulate matter (PM2.5/PM10), temperature, relative humidity, and dew point at equipment-intake height. A 24-hour baseline is recorded before any hardware is energized. Processing is edge-local by design; where consumer-grade sensors default to vendor-cloud reporting, they are network-isolated — firewalled to the local network and read through their local interfaces — before any on-premise claim is made of them.
  • Escalate only on evidence. Alert thresholds trigger a defined ladder: repeat deep clean → filtered positive-pressure supply (MERV-13) → and only then, construction. The partition wall is not cancelled; it is demoted to a remedy the data must ask for.

For context, modern IT hardware tolerates ordinary indoor environments across wide envelopes, and the cleanliness reference commonly cited for data environments is ISO 14644-1 Class 8 — a workaday target far removed from the near-sterile classes of semiconductor fabrication. A sealed, cleaned, climate-controlled room with continuous verification aims to meet that expectation, not to be excused from it. A sealed, cleaned, climate-controlled room with continuous verification meets the operational need without borrowing requirements from an industry we are not in — yet. Should this room’s successor host device-level research with genuine contamination sensitivity, this same monitoring record becomes the baseline that tells us so.

05Verification plan

Continuous consumer-grade optical sensing provides the 24/7 trend record. For periodic verification, the protocol calls for a survey-grade optical particle counter reading true particle counts per cubic meter across 0.3–5 µm channels, operated on a rental or survey basis against the continuous monitor’s readings, checking the room against the ISO 14644-1 Class 8 envelope commonly referenced for data environments. Capital purchase of survey instrumentation is deferred until the baseline record demonstrates a need the continuous monitor cannot meet — the same evidence standard we applied to the wall.

06Limitations

This is a single-facility case note, not a controlled study, and its conclusions are specific to this structure, climate, and duty cycle. It is not engineering advice; local codes and site conditions govern. Baseline and longitudinal data will be appended as collected, including any finding that contradicts the decision documented here. If the data asks for the wall, the wall gets built — that is the entire point of measuring.

Bricks are easy to sell. Measurements are hard to argue with.

What PM2.5 does and does not capture
This sensor measures fine airborne particles (PM2.5). Chemical vapors off-gassing from fresh paint (VOCs) are a separate matter that a particle sensor does not register; ventilation, curing time, and cleaning address those, and a falling particle count is not by itself a claim about them.

Read
Left alone, with every planned intervention still switched off, the room halved its own particle load and closed the day inside the “Good” range. This is the honest starting line the program measures against — recorded before cleaning, filtration, or conditioning, so that any later improvement can be shown rather than asserted.
