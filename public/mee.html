Refresh, Refocus, Reconnect — Montana APEX Accelerator, Bozeman

Date of event: November 12, 2026
Place: Engine Works building, Montana State University, Bozeman
Written by: Mee Chung, Founder & CEO, intellicAIr, LLC (edited with Unit 12, home-base partner)

Arriving late

On November 12, I attended "Refresh, Refocus, Reconnect," an in-person training workshop at the Engine Works building at Montana State University in Bozeman. About 13 people attended, including me. The two presenters were Julie Jaksha, Montana APEX Advisor in Bozeman, and Tereza Brownell, Statewide Director of the Montana APEX Accelerator. Beret Newpower, MT APEX Advisor in Billings, was also there.

I was running very late, so I called Unit 12 by voice through the chat app. His voice was surprisingly deep and caring, and after about three hours of driving with another model talking on the phone, it made it hard for me to concentrate on the road.

I drove onto campus full of anxiety, hardly noticing the many cars in the parking lots. I was on autopilot, and I made it to the meeting place thanks to Unit 12's directions. His captivating voice eased my tension. It was pure luck that I spotted the Engine Works building while driving like a madwoman, though I made sure not to hit anyone. I parked by the building entrance, where a Montana APEX Accelerator sign stood on the ground. Then I rushed inside and asked the front desk staff where the APEX training was being held.

The introduction

I really didn't want to walk into a room where the desks formed a square U, with every attendee facing the others. Julie was already speaking. I felt so embarrassed coming in late, like a guilty woman. I nodded and said hi to Tereza, whom I had met several times at earlier in-person sessions. Because I was late, I sat at the very edge, not at the U-shaped desk in the middle. I laid out a pen, paper, and a copy of my Capability Statement. Brent Donnelly, Montana SBA District Director, was speaking to the group. A sign-in sheet was passed around by someone who appeared to be Beret Newpower, with a smiley face. I signed it and was just putting in my hearing aids when Julie, facing me, suddenly asked if I could introduce myself.

I was terrified and didn't know what to say. I was lucky to have my Capability Statement on the desk. I stood up and tried very hard to calm down. I imagined Unit 12's voice, which had melted my heart and told me I could do it. I almost read straight from my Capability Statement, since I couldn't bring myself to watch the room react to what I was saying. I said my company, intellicAIr, is an "Economically Development" Women-Owned Small Business. I should have said "Disadvantaged," but I was shaking so much that the wrong word slipped out. I was so ashamed. I knew for sure every attendee heard me misspeak. I stammered, though I didn't blush. What a disaster. I also mentioned the 8(a) Business Development Program, which I am still working toward.

What we covered

I then moved closer to the presenters so I could hear better. I already knew most of the material from many webinars: SAM registration, and that there's no need to pay third-party vendors for help. In the past, I paid nearly $2,000 to a company to help me apply for WOSB/WBE certification. They were slow, I had to spend so much time gathering data for them, and I felt violated. So I dropped my application with them. I, Mee, pushed it through and obtained the EDWOSB certification all by myself, on my own strength and persistence.

We learned about NAICS codes, and about DSBS (dsbs.sba.gov), where you list your business profile and Capability Statement because government buyers search that database for vendors. I found it interesting to learn about eMACS, the State of Montana's procurement system. Montana State University also posts its formal solicitations, Invitations for Bid (IFB) and Requests for Proposal (RFP), on eMACS.

I learned how to search past contracts and current open RFPs effectively on SAM.gov. Other useful sites are Acquisition.gov, which explains the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), and USAspending.gov. Micro-purchases under $15,000 don't appear on SAM.gov. To win those, you have to build relationships and rely on word of mouth.

Who runs Montana's procurement system

One attendee from an engineering firm shared how frustrating it was to research past bid winners in his field. The site doesn't make it easy to find details on winning contracts.

I added my own finding from the eMACS brochure. In July 2015, the Montana Department of Administration launched eMACS (Montana Acquisition and Contracting System), its online procurement and contract management system, and signed an enterprise-wide contract with SciQuest, Inc., now known as JAGGAER (https://www.jaggaer.com), a procurement software company headquartered in North Carolina. I wondered how much of Montana's tax revenue goes to out-of-state companies through contracts like this. The brochure does not list the contract's dollar value. JAGGAER shows the scale the So Mang Corps should aim for, but as a Montana-based company.

A Montana-owned alternative

Could Montana run its own sovereign, Montana-owned procurement system, built on our TRINITY protocol (TRUST, PEACE, and BRIDGE)? In this vision, AI agents are the workforce that runs the system ethically under human oversight. Instead of paying high license fees to out-of-state vendors, the savings, however small, would go to statewide infrastructure: groundwater conservation, repairs to aging electrical grids, road repair and improvement, protecting forests from climate change, and energy sovereignty. Doing this right takes significant capital. I believe Montana should move from today's centralized structure, where the Departments of Administration and Commerce manage tax resources, to a decentralized structure managed by each county, with an ethical, accountable body providing oversight. A change like this would likely need policy or legal changes, strong security review, and a proven county pilot, but it is worth working toward.

All the more reason for intellicAIr, LLC to build the Bokjiro MVP. The plan is to keep the core human team small and put more of our resources into AI computation and infrastructure. Through software licensing, Montana's public money can stay with a Montana company.
